> **Act now — every minute counts.** Reporting quickly is the single biggest factor in limiting damage. You will not get in trouble for reporting honestly.

## If you entered your password on a suspicious page

1. **Change your password immediately** from a trusted device, by going to the sign-in page yourself (not via the email link). See "How to reset a forgotten password".
2. If you use the **same password anywhere else**, change it there too.
3. **Deny** any MFA prompts you didn't start.
4. **Open a ticket** — category **Security**, **Impact: Medium or High, Urgency: High** — and also **contact IT directly** by phone or in person.

## If you opened an attachment or ran a file

1. **Disconnect from the network**: turn off Wi-Fi (or unplug the network cable). Don't shut the computer down — IT may need to examine what's running.
2. **Stop using the computer** and don't try to "clean" it yourself.
3. Contact IT immediately and open a ticket from another device (or ask a colleague).

## If you only clicked a link (and entered nothing)

Close the page. The risk is lower, but still **report it** — some pages try to download malware automatically. Open a ticket and use the **Report phishing** button on the original email.

## What to tell IT

- The **time** it happened, as precisely as possible.
- The email itself (use **Report phishing**, or forward it as an attachment).
- What you did: clicked, downloaded, opened, entered password, approved MFA, sent money or data.
- Whether you've already changed your password.

## If money or bank details were involved

If you paid an invoice or changed a supplier's bank details because of the message, **tell your manager and finance immediately** and contact your bank — payments can sometimes be recalled if the bank is told within hours.

## What IT will do

Reset your sessions and password, check sign-in logs, search for the same message in other mailboxes, and scan or isolate your computer. Most incidents reported quickly end with no lasting harm.
